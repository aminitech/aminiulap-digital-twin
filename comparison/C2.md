# C2 — deterministic ray tracing vs the stochastic 3GPP TR 38.901 planning surface

The third and last of the studies pre-registered in
[`benchmarks/METHOD.md`](../benchmarks/METHOD.md). It tests the paper's comparative
claim head-on:

> the deterministic pipeline "upgrades the deployed RF layer from stochastic national
> planning to deterministic site studies"

The paper reports no baseline of any kind, so that sentence has never been checked.
This is the check.

| Script | [`c2_rt_vs_stochastic.py`](c2_rt_vs_stochastic.py) |
| Result | [`results/c2.json`](results/c2.json) |
| Figures | [`figures/c2_rt_vs_stochastic.png`](figures/c2_rt_vs_stochastic.png) · [`figures/c2_serving_cell.png`](figures/c2_serving_cell.png) |

```sh
export ULAP_WORK_DIR=/path/to/rtwork      # holds scene_build/, mitsuba_scene/, mitsuba_scene_terrain/
export MPLBACKEND=Agg
python comparison/c2_rt_vs_stochastic.py
```

Needs Sionna RT 2.0.1 + Mitsuba 3.8.0 + Dr.Jit 1.3.1. The run recorded here was made on
`spark-5804` (NVIDIA GB10, aarch64), Mitsuba variant `cuda_ad_mono_polarized` — read back
from the run, not assumed (METHOD rule 2). **This is a GPU run**; nothing here speaks to
the paper's CPU claim. Scene: **Newton, Barbados**, 576 government footprints with LiDAR
heights, 55.5 m of relief, the two in-scene towers. The bundled open-data `examples/`
scene is not used and the two are never mixed.

---

## What was compared

**Deterministic reference.** Sionna `RadioMapSolver`, 3.5 GHz, 8 m cells, `max_depth` 5,
10⁷ samples/TX, 311 × 295 cells, 3 solver seeds. Two arms:

- **`flat`** *(primary)* — flat Mitsuba scene, TX at z = h, one plane at 1.5 m. Primary
  because TR 38.901 is a flat-earth closed form and this is the like-for-like geometry.
- **`terrain_ground`** — terrain scene, TX at ground_z + h, K = 9 planes with each cell
  read from the plane nearest local terrain + 1.5 m, i.e. what `sionna_terrain_ground.py`
  ships. The 29.0 % of cells whose nearest plane lands *below* local ground — the
  discretisation artifact C3 quantified — are excluded from numerator and denominator.

The maps are **re-solved**, not read from `sionna_out/`: that directory holds rendered
PNGs, not arrays.

**Challenger.** TR 38.901 implemented in the script from the standard, on the *same grid,
same towers, same frequency*, and given **no scene** — a 2-D distance, a mast height and a
1.5 m UE height, which is what a national planning surface has:

| what | where in TR 38.901 | function |
|---|---|---|
| RMa LOS PL1/PL2, d_BP, σ_SF = 4/6 | Table 7.4.1-1 | `rma_pathloss` |
| RMa NLOS PL′, PL = max(LOS, PL′), σ_SF = 8 | Table 7.4.1-1 | `rma_pathloss` |
| UMa LOS PL1/PL2, d′_BP, h_E = 1 m, σ_SF = 4 | Table 7.4.1-1 + Note 1 | `uma_pathloss` |
| UMa NLOS PL′, σ_SF = 6 | Table 7.4.1-1 | `uma_pathloss` |
| Pr(LOS) RMa and UMa (C′(h_UT) = 0 at h_UT = 1.5 m) | Table 7.4.2-1 | `los_probability` |
| SF horizontal correlation distance 37 / 120 m (RMa), 37 / 50 m (UMa) | Table 7.5-6, §7.6.3.1 | `correlated_field` |

Every constant is the value printed in the standard. **Nothing is fitted to the ray
tracer.** RMa is evaluated at the TR's h = 5 m / W = 20 m.

**Scenario choice, justified from the scene, not asserted.** Measured from the manifest:
buildings cover **4.0 %** of the 4.34 km² scene and their mean height is **4.47 m**
(median 4.14 m); the masts are 30 m and 24 m. That is low-rise, sparse, detached
settlement with masts well above clutter — RMa's definition, and the masts sit inside
RMa's 10–150 m window. UMa is run anyway as a declared sensitivity arm so the choice can
be audited. *Declared applicability violation:* Table 7.4.1-1 gives RMa's range as
5 m ≤ h ≤ 50 m and the scene's mean is 4.47 m, marginally below the floor. A sensitivity
at h = 4.47 m is reported; it moves the headline RMS by 0.24 dB.

**Realisations.** Shadow fading and the probabilistic LOS state are random, so a single
draw is not a comparison. **20 seeded realisations** (`SF_BASE_SEED = 20260729`, stream
per realisation/scenario/tower), declared before the first number. Every metric below is
the ensemble median with the min–max range. Re-running the script reproduces every figure
in this document exactly.

**The antenna-units mismatch, handled as C1 handled it.** Sionna's path gain includes the
`tr38901` TX element and `dipole` RX patterns; TR 38.901 path loss is
isotropic-to-isotropic. Both conventions are reported; `antenna_corrected` (the standard's
PL plus the exact Sionna pattern gains, obtained by calling Sionna's own
`v_tr38901_pattern` / `v_dipole_pattern`) is **primary**. This matters far more here than
in C1: the correction is per *cell* and per *tower* — median −7.4 dB at Newton, −20.2 dB
at Rising Sun, 10.9 dB median difference between them — so it moves the serving-tower
decision, not just an offset.

**Design matrix.** 2 RT references × 2 scenarios × 2 LOS-state models × 3 shadow-fading
modes × 2 gain conventions = **48 cells, all run, all in the JSON**. `failures` is empty.

## What this means, including the parts that are inconvenient

### 1. The two do **not** agree, and the paper's "upgrade" claim survives this test — narrowly, and not for the reason the paper implies

METHOD required this study to expose the case where "the two agree within noise on median
path gain", which would weaken the claim. That is not what happened. On the primary cell
the surfaces are **8.95 dB apart on the median**, **20.0 dB RMS**, correlated at only
**r = 0.65**, and **55.8 % of cells differ by more than 10 dB**. TR 38.901 is
systematically **pessimistic by ~12.7 dB** against the ray tracer on this scene.

But *disagreement is not vindication*. There is no drive test, no measurement campaign and
no published dataset anywhere in this directory. This study shows the two methods produce
different maps; it does **not** show the ray-traced one is closer to reality. TR 38.901 is
the one of the two with an empirical pedigree. The honest statement of the finding is:
**deterministic RT measurably changes the planning surface; whether it improves it is not
established here and cannot be established without measurements.**

### 2. Roughly half the disagreement is the LOS *state* model, not the path-loss formulas

Give TR 38.901 the ray tracer's own LOS oracle (a LOS-only solve: `max_depth=0`,
reflection/refraction off) instead of drawing LOS from Table 7.4.2-1, and everything
improves at once:

| | probabilistic LOS | geometric LOS | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| RMS | 19.99 dB | 10.96 dB | **−45 %** |
| bias | −12.69 dB | −5.34 dB | −58 % |
| Pearson r | 0.648 | 0.855 | +0.21 |
| cells > 10 dB | 55.8 % | 20.5 % | −35 pp |
| serving-tower disagreement | 29.4 % | 13.7 % | **−53 %** |

Why: RMa's Pr(LOS) = exp(−(d−10)/1000) predicts a mean LOS probability of **36.2 %**
(Newton) and **33.2 %** (Rising Sun) over this grid; the ray tracer finds **59.6 %** and
**52.0 %**. In a scene that is 4.0 % built, links are clear far more often than a
rural-average curve expects. The middle panel of `c2_serving_cell.png` shows the crossover:
TR 38.901 under-predicts LOS out to ~1.3 km and over-predicts beyond it.

This is the most actionable result in the study. **The largest single defect in the
stochastic surface here is not its propagation mathematics — it is that it does not know
where the buildings are.** That is a much narrower thing to fix than a full deterministic
solve: it needs a visibility answer, not a field solve.

One caveat, against my own argument: the LOS oracle used here is itself a Sionna solve
(`max_depth=0`, reflection/refraction off) and it measured **0.131 s** against 0.023 s
median for the full solve — i.e. *more* expensive, because it is a single cold run that
pays kernel compilation for a new solver configuration and is not amortised over three
repeats. Nothing in this study demonstrates a cheap visibility pass; the claim that one
exists is an inference from what the computation has to do, not a measurement, and it is
labelled as such.

### 3. Even with perfect LOS knowledge, 13.7 % of cells still get the wrong tower

That residual is **63× the RT Monte-Carlo noise floor**. Determinism buys something the
LOS oracle alone does not: which of two masts wins where both are visible.

### 4. The serving-tower churn is not boundary jitter — this is the strongest result in the study

Churn where two servers are within a decibel is harmless; churn where the ray tracer says
one mast is 20 dB stronger is a planning error. Binning by the ray tracer's own
best-vs-second-best margin (primary cell, 20 realisations):

| RT best-vs-2nd margin | cells | serving-tower disagreement |
|---|---|---|
| 0 – 3 dB | 5 280 | 45.3 % *(near coin-flip — expected, harmless)* |
| 3 – 6 dB | 3 788 | 37.9 % |
| 6 – 10 dB | 4 619 | 37.2 % |
| **10 – 20 dB** | **18 076** | **36.6 %** |
| > 20 dB | 14 154 | 9.6 % |
| only one tower visible to the RT at all | 37 365 | 28.7 % |

**Over eighteen thousand cells where the ray tracer says one mast is 10–20 dB stronger,
and the stochastic surface picks the other one 36.6 % of the time.** That is not
handover-boundary noise; that is a neighbour list, a PCI plan and a handover parameter set
built on the wrong mast. If deterministic RT earns its keep anywhere in this study, it is
here.

### 5. Coverage holes — a real structural blindness, but weaker than the obvious framing

The ray tracer finds **9.22 %** of cells with no coverage. The closed form is a continuous
function of distance plus a Gaussian and returns **0 %** by construction. The tempting
headline — "it cannot see holes" — overstates it, and the script reports the corrected
version. Holes cluster at range, so an unconditional comparison flatters the model. Matched
by distance to the nearest tower:

| d_2D bin | holes | served | stochastic median, holes | stochastic median, served | separation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 500 – 750 m | 341 | 19 358 | −126.26 dB | −118.28 dB | −7.99 dB |
| 750 – 1000 m | 812 | 16 431 | −135.73 dB | −125.74 dB | −9.99 dB |
| 1000 – 1500 m | 2 501 | 19 197 | −145.18 dB | −136.91 dB | −8.27 dB |
| 1500 – 2000 m | 3 676 | 6 342 | −153.89 dB | −153.06 dB | **−0.83 dB** |
| > 2000 m | 1 115 | 688 | −156.29 dB | −155.84 dB | **−0.46 dB** |

Out to 1.5 km the model does place hole cells 8–10 dB below served cells in the same bin —
but the only quantities it has inside a bin are residual range and antenna azimuth, so that
separation is *those*, not the obstruction that caused the hole. Beyond 1.5 km, where
**57 % of the ray tracer's holes are**, the separation collapses below 1 dB: hole and
served cells get the same number. The blindness is real; it is not total, and the honest
number is 0.5–1 dB of discrimination where most of the holes live.

### 6. The raw/antenna-corrected trap fires here in the opposite direction to C1 — and it is a trap

| convention | RMS | bias | r | serving-tower |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| antenna-corrected *(primary)* | 19.99 dB | **−12.69 dB** | 0.648 | 29.4 % |
| raw (isotropic) | 16.81 dB | **−1.12 dB** | 0.387 | 38.5 % |

The raw bias of −1.12 dB looks like agreement. It is a coincidence: the antenna correction
shifts the bias by −11.58 dB, which happens to cancel TR 38.901's 12.7 dB pessimism almost
exactly (per-tower median pattern gain −7.4 dB at Newton, −20.2 dB at Rising Sun).
Anyone reporting the raw number would announce that the stochastic model and the ray tracer
agree on median path gain to within a decibel — a conclusion the corrected number
contradicts, and which the raw correlation (r = 0.39 vs 0.65) and the raw serving-tower
disagreement (38.5 % vs 29.4 %) both refuse to support. **`raw` is reported for
completeness and is not the primary convention.**

### 7. Shadow fading barely moves the aggregate metrics, and one of its effects looks backwards

Switching shadow fading off, to i.i.d., or to spatially correlated at the standard's
correlation distances changes the best-server RMS by less than 0.4 dB (20.32 / 20.32 /
19.99) and the serving-tower fraction by less than 1.6 pp (28.0 / 29.6 / 29.4 %). What it
does change is the **ensemble spread**: with SF off the realisation-to-realisation sd of
the RMS is 0.04 dB; with correlated SF it is 0.58 dB.

Adding shadow fading *reduces* the best-server RMS, which looks wrong. It is not: on the
**per-TX** surface, where no maximum is taken, SF increases RMS as expected (24.33 dB
none → 25.22 dB iid). The best-server map takes a max over two towers, and adding noise to
a maximum biases it upward, which cancels part of the −13.5 dB pessimism. Both surfaces
are in the JSON so this can be checked.

The practical consequence: **the correlated-shadow-fading machinery, which is the more
defensible model, is not what is driving any conclusion in this study.** Reported because
it was pre-declared, not because it mattered.

### 8. UMa is badly wrong here, which confirms the scenario choice rather than rescuing it

UMa with probabilistic LOS gives bias −33.2 dB and puts **94.2 %** of cells more than
10 dB from the ray tracer. Its Pr(LOS) averages **2.6 %** over this grid against a measured
59.6 % / 52.0 %. Both scenarios were declared before any result, so this is a sensitivity
outcome, not a selection. It is also a warning about the general practice: the scenario
label is worth about 15 dB of bias on this scene, more than any modelling subtlety in the
study.

### 9. The pre-registered path-loss exponent is the weakest metric here, and is reported as such

n_RT = 1.42, n_stochastic = 4.95, Δn = **+3.53**. But the log-distance fit residual is
**12.1 dB** for the RT surface and **17.9 dB** for the stochastic one. A single
log-distance law is a poor description of *either* 2-D map — the `tr38901` element pattern
varies with azimuth, not distance, and no exponent can absorb that. METHOD fixed the metric
in advance so it is reported in full, with the caveat attached rather than the number
quoted alone.

### 10. Cost — and why the ratio flatters the stochastic model less than expected

| | wall clock |
|---|---|
| RT solve, same grid, median of 3 | **0.0226 s** (cold 0.038 s) |
| TR 38.901, one realisation | **0.00208 s** |
| ratio | RT is **10.9×** more expensive; the closed form is **9.2 %** of the compute |

But the study's own pre-registered rule is that a single stochastic draw is not a
comparison. **Twenty realisations cost 0.042 s — 1.8× a single ray-traced solve.** On this
GPU, once the stochastic model is used honestly, its compute advantage on an equal grid is
gone.

The cost advantage that remains is real and much larger, and this ratio does not capture
it: the RT number excludes LiDAR height extraction, footprint acquisition, terrain
rasterisation and Mitsuba XML export — the entire scene build. The closed form needs none
of it. **The honest cost comparison between these two methods is about data, not FLOPs.**

## Where the stochastic model wins (METHOD rule 7)

Reported as findings in their own right, not as concessions.

1. **It needs no scene.** Three numbers: a distance and two heights. The ray-traced map
   required 576 LiDAR-height footprints, a terrain raster and a Mitsuba export. Over
   territory with no building data — which is most territory, and precisely the setting the
   paper's sovereignty argument addresses — the closed form is not merely cheaper, it is the
   only one of the two that can be run at all.
2. **Its inputs cannot be wrong in an interesting way.** The RT map inherits every error in
   the footprints, the LiDAR heights, the material assignment and the terrain raster. None
   of those are validated anywhere in this repository. C3 already found one such error large
   enough to mis-state a coverage-hole fraction by a factor of four.
3. **It carries an empirical pedigree the deterministic solve does not.** TR 38.901 is
   fitted to measurement campaigns and is designed to reproduce a *distribution* over an
   ensemble of sites. Nothing here demonstrates the ray tracer is closer to any measurement.
   For a national planning surface — an aggregate over many sites — that is the model with
   the stronger claim to statistical validity, and the study's finding that they disagree
   does not change that.
4. **Its uncertainty is explicit.** It ships a σ. The ray-traced map ships a single number
   with no error bar at all; its only quantified uncertainty in this entire directory is a
   0.2 % Monte-Carlo seed effect, which is a property of the estimator, not of the physics.

## Limitations (all of them)

- **Uncalibrated, and this is the load-bearing limitation.** No drive test, no measurement
  campaign, no published dataset. C2 compares two *models*. Their disagreement is evidence
  that they are different, not that either is right. Every "RT says / TR 38.901 says"
  in this document should be read that way.
- **Two towers, one frequency, one scene, one polarisation.** 1×1 V-polarised, one
  `tr38901` element at the TX and one `dipole` at the RX, no downtilt, no sector pattern,
  no MIMO. A real sector antenna would change every number, and it would change the
  serving-tower numbers most of all.
- **The antenna correction is applied to a model that has no antenna.** Adding Sionna's
  pattern gains to a closed-form path loss is the only way to compare the two at all, but it
  imports the ray tracer's antenna assumption into the challenger. The `raw` arm is the
  alternative and it is worse in every respect except bias, for the coincidental reason in
  finding 6.
- **`geometric` LOS is not a TR 38.901 configuration.** It is a diagnostic that violates
  the standard's stochastic design in order to isolate the LOS-state term. Its numbers must
  not be quoted as "TR 38.901 performance".
- **RMa is evaluated 0.53 m below its stated building-height applicability floor.** Declared
  in the JSON; the h = 4.47 m sensitivity moves RMS by 0.24 dB and the serving-tower
  fraction by 0.03 pp, so it is not load-bearing — but the model is being used slightly
  outside its range.
- **d_2D is clipped to the standard's 10 m floor**, which affects a handful of cells at each
  mast.
- **The `terrain_ground` arm excludes 29.0 % of cells** as the C3 underground artifact, and
  the stochastic model is given the *same* scene-free geometry there as in the flat arm
  (mast height above local ground, horizontal distance). It is not given a DEM. That is
  deliberate — a national planning surface does not have one — but it means the
  `terrain_ground` comparison mixes a terrain-aware reference with a terrain-blind
  challenger, and its numbers are not a terrain ablation. C3 owns that question.
- **20 realisations.** Enough for the reported sds (all ≤ 0.6 dB / 0.8 pp) to be tight, not
  enough to characterise the tails of the shadow-fading distribution.
- **GPU only.** `cuda_ad_mono_polarized` throughout. No CPU/LLVM cell was run, so nothing
  here supports or refutes the paper's CPU-sovereignty claim.
- **Timing is indicative.** Dr.Jit caches compiled kernels on disk; "cold" means first solve
  in a fresh interpreter. `benchmarks/` owns the authoritative harness.
- **No failed cells.** All 48 ran; `failures` is empty in the JSON. That is an outcome, not
  an absence of the mechanism — the script records failures rather than dropping them
  (METHOD rule 5).

## Verdict

**Does deterministic ray tracing measurably improve on stochastic TR 38.901 planning on
this scene?** It measurably *differs* — by 20.0 dB RMS, 12.7 dB of bias and a different
serving tower in 29.4 % of cells, 135× the ray tracer's own noise floor — and the
difference is concentrated exactly where planning decisions get made: **28 dB at the cell
edge**, **36.6 % wrong-mast selection across 18 076 cells the ray tracer says are 10–20 dB
decided**, and 9.2 % of the map called a coverage hole by one method and nothing at all by
the other. On median path gain over a whole map, which is what a coverage summary usually
quotes, the gap is a more modest 8.95 dB.

Against that: roughly **half** the disagreement comes from one term — the LOS state — which
a visibility pass fixes at a fraction of a full solve; the shadow-fading model that
motivates most of the "stochastic" label changes almost nothing; and **no measurement
anywhere in this repository establishes which of the two maps is closer to the radio.** The
paper's sentence should be narrowed from "upgrades" to something it can support:
*deterministic ray tracing changes serving-cell association and cell-edge levels far beyond
solver noise, and identifies coverage holes the stochastic surface cannot represent; it has
not been shown to be more accurate.*
